Orientation brief

Inside AI-Native Mortgage

A walk through the Coforge mortgage origination design — what the process does, what the demo is actually showing you, how it is built, and what it would take to run it on real loans.

Reference: Coforge AI-Native Mortgage Demo.html MVP: mvp/index.html
Read this before anything else

Neither the reference design nor the MVP contains any AI. No model runs, no document is read, no loan data leaves the file. Every exception you will see is hardcoded, with its confidence score and its routing decision written in advance. The demo is a deterministic simulation — it plays the same script every time.

That is the correct thing for it to be. Its job is to specify the behaviour the real system must have, and to let people see and argue with that behaviour before anyone builds it. But if you present it to a stakeholder who believes they are watching a working system, you will have a credibility problem later. Say "this is the design, running" — not "this is the product".

Part 01

The end-to-end process, and what it is for

A mortgage takes roughly six weeks to originate. Very little of that is thinking. Most of it is fetching documents, checking them against rulebooks, noticing what is missing or contradictory, and chasing it down.

The premise of this solution is that the fetching-and-checking is machine work, and the judgment is human work — and that today those two are tangled together in the same person's inbox. Separate them, and you get speed without giving up control.

The stated goal is a cycle time falling from a 43-day baseline toward the high twenties, achieved not by making people work faster but by never putting mechanical work in front of them in the first place.

The five stages

A loan moves through five stages. The MVP covers the first three, which is where the interesting decisions live; the last two are in the reference design and are stubbed for later.

Stage 1
Application & Intake
Documents arrive, are classified, and borrower and income fields are extracted.
Stage 2
Loan Processing
Title, appraisal, flood and credit are ordered; the file is checked against the rules engine.
Stage 3
Underwriting
Automated underwriting runs, risk is scored, and a human underwriter decides.
Stage 4
Closing & Funding
Disclosure reconciliation, escrow, eSign, wire. In the reference only.
Stage 5
Post-Closing QC
Indexing, quality control, investor delivery. In the reference only.

The one idea that matters: disposition

Everything the system finds wrong with a loan is called an exception — a missing document, a number that does not reconcile, a ratio outside policy, a lien on title. Every exception is immediately assigned a disposition, and that single field decides whether a machine or a person handles it.

Exception found → confidence + severity → disposition
disp: auto
The machine fixes it

High confidence, mechanical remedy, no judgment required. The agent performs the repair and closes the exception itself. Nobody is interrupted.

Re-run the OCR at higher resolution. Order the flood certificate. Request the missing bank statement.

disp: hitl
A person decides

Human-in-the-loop. The agent has done the analysis and has a recommendation, but the call is a judgment, so it routes to a named analyst's queue with its evidence attached.

  1. Analyst resolves it — verify, recalculate, request a document, or override.
  2. If the exception is marked requiresSup, the analyst may only propose. A supervisor signs off before anything is applied.

Two things are worth pausing on, because they are the difference between this and a workflow tool:

  • The system decides what it cannot decide. Disposition is the machine making a judgment about the limits of its own competence. That is the actual product.
  • High confidence does not mean automatic. The FHA debt-to-income breach in the demo sits at 79% confidence — the machine is quite sure of the finding — and it still goes to a human, because weighing compensating factors against a policy cap is not a thing you let software do quietly.

Who is allowed to do what

Three human roles, with genuinely different authority. This is not decoration — it is enforced in the code, and every crossing is recorded.

Analyst ×3
Owns one queue (A, B or C). Resolves cases in their own queue only. Cannot approve their own sign-off items, and cannot see another analyst's work.
Supervisor
Sees every queue. Approves or rejects proposed interventions, and can reassign a case between analysts. Cannot render a credit decision.
Underwriter
Works only in the Underwriters' Digital Hub, on files the agents have already cleaned. Renders the final decision. Does not work the queues.

How a file finishes

A loan becomes Ready for Underwriting the moment every exception at stages 1–3 is resolved or approved. It is a computed condition, re-evaluated after every single action — not a status somebody sets. When it flips, the file appears in the Underwriters' Hub with its guideline flags, AUS result, conditions checklist and document summaries already assembled. The underwriter approves, approves with conditions, suspends, or denies.

And every step of that — each prediction, each repair, each routing decision, each approval, each override — writes a timestamped line to an audit trail tagged as either AI or Human. In a regulated business that record is not a feature; it is the price of admission.

Part 02

What one run produces

48 scripted events. Three exceptions repaired by machine — 100% of the auto lane. Seven routed to human queues. Two loans reach the Hub before the scan even finishes. Straight-through rate lands at 33%: two of the six loans never needed a person at all.

Be careful with these numbers

33% straight-through and a 41-day cycle time are properties of a dataset someone chose, not measurements of anything. They exist to make the mechanism legible. If a stakeholder writes them into a business case, that is a misunderstanding worth correcting in the room.

The demo path, in order

  1. Sign in as analyst1 (password Coforge@123). Press Scan for New Mortgage Loan Requests.
  2. Watch the banner and the live log. Named agents narrate their own work as it happens. Toasts fire as cases route to queues. Use Step to advance one event at a time if you want to talk over it — that control exists precisely for presenting.
  3. Two loans go green mid-scan. Emily Davis and the Thompsons arrive at the Hub without a human touching them. That is the payoff moment; let it land.
  4. Open AI Exceptions & HITL. You see only Queue A. Not a filter — that is the whole screen for an analyst.
  5. Work a case. Every card shows the evidence, the AI's rationale, its recommendation and its confidence. Try Override: it will not submit without a written note. The system will not let a human silently contradict the machine either.
  6. Hit the DTI case. The only button is Propose Fix → Supervisor. This is the moment to explain requiresSup.
  7. Switch to supervisor using the role selector. An Approvals tab appears that did not exist a second ago. Approve the proposal and watch the loan advance.
  8. Switch to underwriter. Guideline flags against program caps, the AUS finding, a conditions checklist where agent-cleared items are locked, GenAI document summaries. Render the decision.
  9. Finish on the Audit Trail. Filter by AI, then by Human. Every action, attributed. For a risk or compliance audience, start here instead.

Part 03

The tech stack

There are two artefacts, built very differently and for different reasons.

The reference design

Coforge AI-Native Mortgage Demo.html — 886 KB, produced in a visual design canvas rather than hand-written.

React 18.3.1
Loaded as UMD bundles; the app is one large class Component extends DCLogic, roughly 137 KB of logic.
Design system
A bundled component library — OnboardXSecure — providing Card, StatCard, Badge, Tag, Avatar, ProgressBar, SidebarItem and ten more.
Packaging
A self-extracting bundle: every asset gzipped, base64'd into a manifest, and unpacked by a loader script on open. This is why the file cannot be read directly in an editor.
Typography
Poppins, Mulish and JetBrains Mono, embedded as woff2.

The MVP

mvp/index.html — 97 KB, one file, deliberately primitive.

  • No dependencies at all. Vanilla JavaScript. No React, no npm, no build step, no bundler, no CDN scripts. The only external request is the Google Fonts stylesheet.
  • Opens by double-click, works offline, survives being emailed. That is the entire reason for the constraint. A demo that needs a dev server is a demo that fails in somebody's boardroom.
  • One App object holding state, with screens that return HTML strings and a single delegated event handler.
  • The reference's design system, re-implemented in plain CSS at the same measurements — including its 21-glyph icon set, ported path by path.
What is not in the stack

No backend. No database. No API. No language model. No document storage. No integration with any loan origination system. Both artefacts are front-ends with their data compiled in.

Part 04

How the solution is built underneath

The simulation is a small state machine driven by a queue of events. Understanding it takes about five minutes, and it is worth the five minutes, because the production system should keep this shape.

The engine

Pressing scan calls buildScanQueue(), which walks every loan across stages 1–3 and emits a flat list of events. Playback then steps through that list on a timer, applying one event at a time:

reveal
A loan is ingested. Document Intake Agent logs the borrower, program and amount.
stage
The loan enters a stage. The agent responsible for that stage narrates what it is doing.
predict
Validation Agent finds an exception and records its severity and confidence.
repair → resolve-auto
auto lane Processing Agent applies the fix; Validation Agent confirms and closes it.
route
human lane Workflow Orchestration Agent sends the case to the correct analyst queue.
settle
The run ends and readiness is evaluated across the book.

Four decisions worth carrying forward

It is deterministic on purpose

No randomness, and no wall clock inside the engine — timestamps come from a synthetic clock that starts at 09:12:00 every run. The same demo happens every time, which matters when you are presenting, and matters more when you are debugging.

Permissions live in the state layer, not the buttons

Hiding a control is not a control. Every function that changes anything re-checks the role before it writes: an analyst cannot touch another queue's case even by calling the function directly, a supervisor cannot render a credit decision, an underwriter cannot work a queue. This is deliberate rehearsal for the production rule — the tool refuses, not the interface — because an LLM-driven agent will eventually be the thing calling these functions, and it will not be looking at your buttons.

Readiness is derived, never assigned

Nothing marks a loan ready. A rule re-runs after every action and decides. Derived state cannot drift out of sync with the facts underneath it.

Nothing changes without an audit line

Every mutation writes to both the live log and the audit trail, tagged AI or human. It is an invariant, not a convention.

The agents, as currently defined

Seven named agents. Today each one is a label on log lines — but the naming is the specification, and each becomes a real agent with a prompt, a toolset and an escalation rule.

Alongside the code there is a skill file at .claude/skills/ai-native-mortgage/ holding the domain model, the agent roster, the design tokens, the full seed dataset and the conventions above — so the next stage of work builds on the same vocabulary rather than reinventing it.

Part 05

What production would actually require

This is the honest part. Going from this design to a system that touches real loans is not a matter of connecting an API. Most of the work has not started.

Replace simulated agents with real ones

Each of the seven becomes an LLM-backed agent with a system prompt, a defined toolset, and an explicit escalation rule. Document extraction pairs a document-intelligence service with a model that reasons over what it extracted. Validation pairs a deterministic rules engine — agency guidelines are rules, and rules should be code — with a model for the ambiguity the rules do not cover.

The single highest-leverage decision in the build

Today, confidence scores are typed into the seed data. In production a model produces them, and the threshold separating auto from hitl becomes the most consequential number in the system. Set it too high and you have automated nothing. Set it too low and the machine is quietly making decisions it should not. That threshold needs a labelled evaluation set and measured precision per exception type — not a guess, and never one global number.

Build the tool layer

Agents act through tools, and each tool declares its side effects and whether it requires human confirmation: read and write loan data in the origination system; retrieve and store documents; submit to automated underwriting; order title, appraisal, flood and credit from vendors; evaluate the rules engine; send borrower outreach. Anything that spends money or touches a borrower is confirmation-gated by default.

Make the orchestration durable

An in-memory event queue is fine for 48 events over 30 seconds. A real loan lives for six weeks, spans dozens of human handoffs, and must survive deployments. That means a durable workflow engine, real work queues with assignment and SLA timers and escalation, an append-only audit store, a relational store for loan and exception state, and an object store for documents.

Satisfy the regulatory surface

This is lending, so this section is not optional and is larger than the engineering.

  • Sensitive personal data throughout — social security numbers, income, bank statements. Encryption in transit and at rest, field-level for the worst of it, and strict handling of what is sent to a model provider.
  • Adverse action reasoning. If a loan is declined, the borrower is entitled to specific principal reasons. Any AI contribution to that path must be explainable in those terms, or must be kept out of that path.
  • Fair lending. The largest exposure. Anything influencing credit outcomes needs bias testing before launch and disparate-impact monitoring after it, on live decisions.
  • Model risk governance. A model inventory, independent validation, ongoing performance monitoring, and a documented owner — the same discipline any other credit model carries.

The design already gets the most important thing right, and it should be defended in every review: no agent renders a credit decision. Agents prepare files. People decide.

Measure it before trusting it

Assemble a golden set of historical loans with known outcomes, and track: auto-repair precision — how often an automatic fix was wrong, the number that should keep you up at night; routing recall — how often something that needed a human did not get one; extraction accuracy field by field; analyst handling time; and end-to-end cycle time against a measured baseline rather than an assumed one.

A rollout that earns trust in order

Done
Design and demonstrable MVP

The behaviour is specified, visible, and arguable. Where we are today.

Phase 1
Shadow mode

Agents run beside the existing team on live files. Nobody acts on their output. Compare agent findings to what humans actually did, and calibrate confidence against that.

Phase 2
Routing and queues, no automation

Agents predict and route; every case still goes to a person. This alone removes triage work and proves the routing before any automatic action exists.

Phase 3
A narrow auto lane

Automate only the two or three highest-precision mechanical exception types — ordering a flood certificate, requesting a lapsed insurance page. Each behind its own switch, each individually revocable.

Phase 4
Hub assembly

Agents assemble the decision-ready file and draft document summaries. Advisory only — the underwriter still reads the source.

Phase 5
Widen on evidence

Expand the auto lane exception type by exception type, each promotion justified by measured precision. Extend into closing and post-closing.

Part 06

Where this sits, and what it changes for people

Automation has come to mortgage operations twice already. Understanding why those waves stalled explains what is different here.

First wave
Robotic process automation. Scripts clicking through screens. Fast to build, brittle by nature — a vendor changes a PDF layout and the robot breaks silently.
Second wave
Point machine learning. A model for document classification, another for extraction. Genuinely useful and narrow. Each one still hands a human a pile of output to reconcile.
This design
Agentic. Agents that reason across documents and rulebooks, act through tools, and — the part that matters — recognise the edge of their own competence and hand off with the evidence attached.

The distinguishing capability is not that it reads documents. It is that it knows which findings it should not act on, and routes those to the right person with its reasoning shown. A workflow tool routes what you told it to route. This decides what routing is warranted.

Human-in-the-loop is the product, not a limitation

It is tempting to treat the human queues as scaffolding to be removed once the models improve. That reading is wrong on two counts. Legally, full autonomy over credit decisions is not obviously permissible and certainly not defensible today. Commercially, the value is already captured in the confident majority — routing the remainder correctly and fast is the differentiator, not a consolation.

Trust is built by making reasoning inspectable. Every exception card carries the evidence snapshot, the rationale, the recommendation and the confidence figure — the human sees what the machine saw, and can disagree with it on the record.

What changes for each stakeholder

Borrower
Closes sooner, and is asked for the same document fewer times.
Loan officer
Fewer files stalling invisibly; earlier warning when one is in trouble.
Pre-underwriting analyst
Stops chasing paper and starts working exceptions that need reasoning. Worth saying plainly in the room: this design redirects the role, it does not delete it.
Underwriter
Receives assembled, decision-ready files instead of raw ones. Their scarce time goes to the decision rather than to preparing for it.
Supervisor
Live queue load and SLA ageing, the ability to rebalance, and a sign-off gate on everything consequential.
Risk & compliance
A complete, attributable record of who — or what — did each thing, and when. For this audience the audit trail is the product.
Operations leadership
Cost per loan and cycle time move without adding headcount; capacity becomes elastic against volume swings.
Investors & regulators
Provenance for every decision in the file, which is increasingly what diligence actually asks for.

Risks worth naming out loud

  • Confident wrong extraction is worse than no extraction. Every extracted field must be traceable to a location in a source document, so a human can check it in seconds rather than re-reading the file.
  • Automation bias. Show people a recommendation and they approve it. The countermeasure is interface design — lead with the evidence, never pre-select the decision, and require a written note for an override. The MVP already does the last of these.
  • Fair lending exposure compounds silently and surfaces in an examination. Monitoring belongs in the first production release, not a later one.
  • Concentration risk on a single model provider, for a process that cannot stop.

Where it goes next

Confidence thresholds that tune themselves as outcome data accumulates. Agents that surface recurring guideline ambiguities back to the policy team. A borrower-facing agent that collects documents conversationally instead of by email. And the two remaining stages — closing and post-closing — which the reference design has already mapped and which are waiting to be built.
